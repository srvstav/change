    turn

    #The announcement

    "You're at the clinic. Your paper says 47. They're on 31. Is that ten minutes or two hours? You don't know, so you sit on a plastic chair and stare at a door. turn is a QR code taped to the counter. Scan it: no app, no sign-up, no phone number. Your phone shows your place, live: 'You're 16th. About 25–40 min. Go get chai.' It buzzes when you're 3 away. The person at the counter just taps next. Nobody shouts your name, and you never have to ask 'is it my turn yet?' It works for clinics, barbers, passport offices, ration shops, college fee counters, and the food stall at a stadium. It takes ten seconds to set up and the first counter is free."

    #Problem

    • People lose hours in physical lines, and it's worse than lost time: you can't leave, because you might miss your turn, and you can't know how long it will be. Clinic waiting rooms (OPDs) in India, government offices, banks, barbers, repair shops, campus offices.
    • Small places run on paper tokens, shouting, or "who's last?". The staff get the same question 50 times a day: "how long?"
    • Proper queue software exists (Qminder, Waitwhile, Qless). It's built for big chains: monthly subscriptions, staff accounts, and an SMS to your phone number, which then gets spam. A barber or a small clinic will never set that up.
    • Shy people suffer most: they don't want their name called out, and they don't want to keep asking.

    #Insight

    • What people hate isn't the waiting. It's not knowing, and being stuck to one spot. Show the real place in line and an honest time range, and the wait becomes free time: errands, chai, a walk.
    • Privacy is the feature, not a policy. No name, no number, no account. Your ticket is a signed token that lives in your phone's browser. The server only knows "ticket 47 in queue X". That's why a shy person scans it without a second thought, and why it will feel safer than anything that asks for a phone number.
    • Setup has to take no effort. The counter is any phone: open the site, "start a queue", show or print the QR. If it takes longer than lighting a lamp, small shops won't do it.
    • The ETA is honest and learns as it goes. It times the real gaps between "next" taps and shows a range that narrows as the day goes on ("25–40 min" → "30–35 min"). It never shows one fake-precise number.
    • The same tech, finally doing something that matters. One "next" tap = one publish → every waiting phone gets its new position, across as many servers as needed. It's exactly the pattern from the websockets repo and nod, with a reason for anyone to use it.

    #Design sketch

    counter phone:   [ next ]  [ skip ]  [ pause ]      big now-serving number, live waiting count
waiting phone:   "You're 16th · about 25–40 min · we'll buzz you at 3 away"   [ I'm leaving the line ]
optional TV:     now serving 31 · 32 · 33          (any browser on a TV)

server:   queue state in Redis (tiny: counters + an ordered set of ticket ids; no personal data)
          "next" → publish queue:<id> {serving: 32}  → every server pushes the new position to its phones (ws)
buzz:     Web Push for a locked phone (Android Chrome: works; iPhone: only when added to the Home Screen, iOS 16.4+)
          + vibrate and sound when the page is open + it re-syncs the instant the tab wakes up
tickets:  signed id stored in localStorage, tied to that browser, so it can't be copied to jump the line

    #Done when

    1. A shopkeeper with no account goes from opening the site to a QR on screen in under 30 seconds.
    2. A customer scans and sees their place in line within 3 s on a 4G phone. They're never asked for a name or number.
    3. With 500 people in one queue spread over 2 servers, a "next" tap updates every phone within 1 s (p95).
    4. Android Chrome, phone locked in a pocket: the "3 away" buzz arrives 19 times out of 20.
    5. A copied or edited ticket link is rejected. Two phones can't hold the same place.

    #Time box

    2 weeks to a version that one real place uses for a full day. If it runs over, cut the TV screen and the ETA ranges. Don't cut the buzz.

    #Riskiest assumption

    Two, in this order:

    1. The buzz reaches a locked phone, especially an iPhone, which only allows Web Push for sites added to the Home Screen. If it doesn't arrive, people won't dare walk away, and the whole point is gone.
    2. Counter staff keep tapping "next" on a busy day. If they forget, every phone shows a lie.

    Test (one afternoon): run a rough version at one real place: a barber, a campus canteen, a small clinic. Watch: what % of people scan; what % walk away and come back on time; how many buzzes arrive; whether staff are still tapping after 2 hours. Kill or rethink if staff stop tapping, or if fewer than half the locked-phone buzzes arrive.

    #Not doing

    • Booking ahead or appointments. That's a different product (calendars, no-shows).
    • Collecting phone numbers, names or emails, ever. If SMS is ever needed, the customer types the number themselves, it's used once and then deleted.
    • Ads on the waiting screen.
    • An app. It's a web page, or it doesn't get used.

    #Open questions

    • How does it earn money? Free for one counter; a small monthly fee for several counters, the TV screen and "average wait by hour" charts (counts only, never people)?
    • Should the waiting screen carry nod ("38 others waiting with you")? It costs nothing and makes the wait feel shared. Keep it off by default.

    #Runner-up

    • working?: QR stickers on shared things that break (an ATM with no cash, a broken lift, a water cooler, a hostel washing machine). Scan to see its live status and how long ago someone last checked; one tap to update it. It saves the wasted walk. It's the same tech, but needs a crowd to keep it fresh.
