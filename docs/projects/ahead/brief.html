    projects / ahead · updated 2026-10-21

    ahead

    #The announcement

    "You'll never look anything up again. ahead reads your college mail, calendar, portal, clubs and chats overnight. Every morning, before you think to ask, a two-minute brief is waiting for you. It covers what's due, what changed, what you promised someone, and the perk that expires Friday. A timeline shows your next 14 days with clashes in red. An attendance bar tells you that you can miss two more Physics classes, and no more. Any time of day, ask it 'can I skip tomorrow?' or 'when's the fest?' and get an answer from your own life, not from a search. Setup is one login. Everything else it leaves out, on purpose."

    #Problem

    Students, and every employee, have their life spread across 6 to 10 places: mail, WhatsApp groups, college portal, club pages, calendar, notice boards. Keeping up means checking each one by hand. People still miss deadlines, attendance cutoffs, events and perks, and forget what they promised in chats. They cope by scrolling everything, asking friends "what did I miss?", and luck. The user's words: "I hate lookups, everyone hates lookups."

    #Insight

    • The pain is dropped balls, not missing information. Showing "all updates" is just a lookup with extra steps. The value is in what gets left out, ranked by "would I regret missing this?"
    • It has two modes, and both matter. Push: the brief shows up daily without anyone asking. Pull: it's always there to answer a question, so it replaces opening other apps. People who look things up end up coming here.
    • Why existing tools miss: OpenClaw and other agents can do this in theory, but they need a long setup, have no built-in judgment, and don't connect to campus systems. Google Now and Copilot never got into the places where student life actually happens (portals, WhatsApp).
    • Why now: models are good enough at judgment to rank a day's noise in seconds. Reading is cheap (Claude Haiku 5.5) and writing is good (Claude Sonnet/Opus 5.5).
    • Visuals only where they change what you do: deadline timeline, attendance meter, promises board, use-it-or-lose-it perks.

    #What makes it great, not just fine

    Siri, Bixby and Gemini fail because they have no context, no judgment, and they wait to be asked. OpenClaw has context only after a heavy setup, has no judgment, and is risky. ahead wins only if all five hold:

    1. A life ledger, not search. Every message is turned into facts: deadlines, events, promises (who owes whom), attendance counts, perks with expiry dates, people. The brief, answers and alerts are all computed from the ledger. Answers come instantly and stay consistent.
    2. Every fact has a receipt. "Lab moved to 204," with a link to the message it came from. When it isn't sure, it says so. It never guesses silently. (Same idea as glass: context with receipts.)
    3. The right moment, not just the morning. Alerts tied to time and place ("lab moved, leave now"), under a strict daily limit of pings. It learns from what you ignore.
    4. Privacy you can check, not privacy we promise. Sessions and the ledger live on your device. Only small snippets go to the model. The companion app is open source.
    5. Correctness is measured. Every miss gets logged and becomes a test. We only claim what the numbers show. "Never miss a deadline" goes on the page only after 14 days of zero misses.

    Later: one-tap actions taken straight from the ledger (draft the reply, add the event, fill the form), always with a yes from the user.

    #Done when

    • With one login, the user gets a brief by 7am, 14 days in a row, without asking.
    • The brief takes under 2 minutes to read and has at most 10 items.
    • Over 14 days, the user misses 0 deadlines or events they'd have regretted missing. Check by logging every miss.
    • Over 14 days, the user opens the college portal and mail by hand on 3 days or fewer. Check with screen time.
    • Asking a question ("when's my next deadline?") gets a correct answer in under 10 seconds, 9 out of 10 times on a fixed list of 10 questions.

    #How we measure

    • Ledger accuracy (built first). Pick 3 real days. By hand, list every fact that should have been caught: deadlines, events, promises, changes, perks. Run the extractor on the same days. Count what it caught (target ≥ 95%) and what it made up (target 0 facts without a real source).
    • Brief quality. Each morning for 14 days, mark each item "mattered" or "noise", and log anything missed. Target: 0 misses, ≤ 2 noise items a day.
    • Ask. A fixed list of 10 questions with known answers. Target: 9 of 10 correct, each citing the fact it came from.
    • Every miss becomes a test case in ~/code/ahead/evals/.

    #Simplest version

    One command on my laptop, no connectors yet:

    1. Import a dump (WhatsApp chat export, or JSON lines from any source) into a local SQLite ledger.
    2. Claude turns the messages into facts. Each fact links back to the messages it came from.
    3. Rank the facts by "would I regret missing this?", with at most 10 items.
    4. Write one HTML brief: today's items, a 14-day timeline, and promises. Each item has its receipt.
    5. ahead ask "…" answers only from the ledger and cites the facts it used.

    This proves the ledger, receipts and ranking end to end. After that: Gmail and Calendar (official, read-only), then a portal browser extension, then WhatsApp linked device, then the 7am schedule.

    #Time box

    Two weeks to a self-test v1: mail, calendar and portal for one user (me). If the numbers above aren't met, park it.

    #Riskiest assumption

    That the sources can be reached reliably with one login. Campus portals have no APIs and often use captchas or OTPs. Personal WhatsApp has no official API. If access breaks weekly, nothing else matters.

    • Test (one afternoon): write a script that logs into my own college portal and pulls attendance and notices. Run it 3 times across a day. Separately, export one WhatsApp group chat and see if the data is usable.
    • Second test (one hour): paste yesterday's real mail, chats and portal notices into Claude and ask for a "would I regret missing this?" brief. Judge whether it beats my own scrolling. If it doesn't, the ranking is the problem, not access.

    #Access: one tap per source, then never again

    • WhatsApp: link it as a device by scanning a QR code once, the same way WhatsApp Web works. Unofficial libraries do this (Baileys, whatsmeow). This breaks WhatsApp's terms, so there's a ban risk. Keep it read-only, and keep the session on the user's own device. On Android, a safer fallback is to read incoming WhatsApp notifications after one permission toggle.
    • Portal and club sites: a browser extension that uses the user's own logged-in session. No passwords stored, no captcha fights.
    • Gmail and Calendar: official Google sign-in, read-only. A public launch needs Google's security review for Gmail access.
    • Rule: sessions stay on the user's device. Only the extracted text goes to Claude.

    #Safety

    • Read-only access everywhere. No tools to send, delete or pay.
    • The reader that sees untrusted text has no tools. Anything that acts only sees summaries and waits for a yes.
    • The model name lives in one config line, with a test set of 30+ real past days to check before any model upgrade.

    #Not doing

    • No acting on your behalf in v1 (no replies, no form-filling, no RSVPs).
    • No general agent framework, no plugin store.
    • No multi-college support until one campus works.
    • No charts that don't change what you do today.
    • No sending from WhatsApp, ever, since sending is what gets accounts banned. Read only.
    • No WhatsApp sessions held on our servers.

    #Design

    connectors (on device)  ──►  items (raw messages, SQLite)
                                  │  extract: Claude, no tools, structured output
                                  ▼
                             facts (ledger; each fact → source item ids)
                                  │  rank: plain code, testable, max 10
                                  ▼
                      brief.html  ·  ask (answers only from facts, with citations)

    TypeScript on Node, node:sqlite. No native dependencies.
