    projects / sketch · updated 2026-10-21

    sketch

    #The announcement

    "You reread the chapter three times. You highlighted half of it. In the exam, the page is blank. sketch is for the night before — and the exam after. Drop in your lecture slides, a textbook chapter, or your notes, and sketch doesn't summarize them. It finds the shape of the topic and draws it: the Krebs cycle as a factory line where each worker hands off one part, the causes of World War I as a powder keg with four fuses, a proof as a road with the one turn everyone misses. Every picture is tested before you see it. A second AI looks at only the picture and tries to answer real exam-style questions about your material. If it can't, sketch redraws. Then sketch hides pieces of the picture and asks you to fill them in. In the exam, you don't remember the words. You remember the picture, and the words come back with it."

    #Problem

    • Who: university and senior school students who are studying for an exam on material they were given (slides, chapters, notes). They have a lot of it and a deadline.
    • How bad: the most common study habits are rereading and highlighting. They feel productive but they don't help you recall. Students find this out in the exam room, when it's too late.
    • How they cope today:
      • Anki / Quizlet: good for facts, bad for structure. Breaking a topic into 200 cards loses how the parts fit.
      • NotebookLM, ChatGPT: summaries, mind maps, podcasts. The mind maps are generic trees that all look the same. Nothing checks that the output actually holds the idea.
      • Hand-drawn mind maps and diagrams: these work, but they're slow and most students can't draw well or don't know which shape to draw.
      • YouTube explainers: great when one exists for your exact topic. Often it doesn't, or it doesn't match your course.

    #Insight

    • The picture must carry the structure, not decorate it. A process becomes a machine, a trade-off a seesaw, a history a road, a classification a map, a cause a chain of dominoes. Picking the right shape for this idea is the hard, thoughtful step, and it's exactly what a strong model can now do within one context window.
    • The eval loop is the product. "Can a fresh model answer exam questions about the material from the picture alone?" is a cheap, automatic test of whether the picture holds the idea. Generate → quiz the picture → find what's missing → redraw. Nobody ships visuals with that check.
    • The student has to do the remembering. Seeing a good picture isn't enough. sketch hides parts of the picture (an empty box, a missing fuse, a blank turn in the road) and the student fills them in. Recalling from the picture is what ties the words to the image.
    • Exams are the perfect first market. There's a clear deadline, a clear test of success (the grade) and students talk to each other. One picture that saves someone's exam gets shared in the class group chat.
    • Why now: models write good SVG/HTML and can judge images in the same loop. Long context means a whole lecture's worth of material fits without RAG.

    #Done when

    • A student who has never seen sketch can drop in a real lecture PDF and get a checked visual set in under 3 minutes.
    • Every visual shown has a picture-only quiz score of 80% or more (4 of 5 questions), and the score is visible.
    • Recall test with 8 students, all using real material from the same course: 4 study with sketch, 4 with their normal method, for the same 30 minutes. Three days later they take a 10-question quiz without notes. The sketch group scores at least 2 points higher on average.
    • At least 3 of those 8 students use it again unprompted for their next exam.

    #How we measure

    Three layers, cheapest first. Build them before tuning prompts.

    1. Unit tests (npm test): the draw → check → redraw loop with a fake model, the SVG cleaner, blank-out and answer grading. Must pass on every change.
    2. Picture-only score (built into every run): 5 exam-style questions per idea, written from the source with an answer key. A judge model sees only the rendered PNG and answers. A grader marks the answers against the key. Pass = 80% or more. Logged per idea, per round, so a prompt change can be compared across the same material.
    3. The human recall test in Done when. This is the one that decides whether sketch lives.

    #Simplest version

    One static web page, no backend. Paste text or drop a PDF / image of notes, add your API key, press Etch. You get 3–8 checked pictures plus an overview, a score on each, blank-out recall, and PNG/SVG/HTML export. Everything is saved in the browser.

    #Time box

    Three weekends for v1: one for the draw + check loop, one for blank-out recall, one for testing with real students. No accounts and no backend in v1. If the recall test doesn't show a gap, stop and rethink the shapes before adding anything.

    #Riskiest assumption

    That model-made, structure-first visuals help students recall more than their normal study method does, not just look nicer.

    Test in an afternoon, no code: take one real lecture (yours or a friend's). In a Claude chat, make (a) a good written summary and (b) 3–4 structure-first visuals as HTML/SVG, iterating by hand. Give (a) to 2 students and (b) to 2 others for 20 minutes. Quiz all 4 two days later. If the visual group isn't clearly better, the shape-picking idea is wrong.

    Second risk: API keys. Most students don't have an API key and won't make one. BYOK works for the test group and for power users. A real launch needs a free tier (a few topics per week on our key) or a cheap student plan. Decide before launch, not before v1.

    #Not doing

    • RAG / whole-semester libraries (later, only after v1 works on one lecture).
    • Full spaced-repetition scheduling, accounts, sync, mobile app.
    • Video, animation, voice.
    • Claims about "memory science". It works or it doesn't. The recall test decides.
    • Storing student keys or material on a server.
    • Non-student audiences (meetings, talks) until students love it.

    #Design

    • Plain ES modules, no build step (same as hush). Hosted on GitHub Pages.
    • Claude is called straight from the browser with the student's own key (dangerouslyAllowBrowser).
    • The source document is the first block of every source-side call, so it's cached once and reused.
    • src/pipeline.js holds the loop and takes the model as an argument, so it's tested without a network.
    • Pictures are SVG. They're cleaned with an allowlist and shown as <img>, so a hostile PDF can't run code.
    • See the code repo's README. Lesson filed: test-pictures-with-a-blind-quiz.
